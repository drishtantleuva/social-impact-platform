A carbon credit counts the tonnes.
It says nothing about the people.

Carbon projects reshape the communities that host them — through employment, health, education and economic growth — yet conventional, carbon-centred evaluation leaves those outcomes unmeasured, and sometimes reproduces social injustice. This research quantifies that missing half: the social impact of carbon-credit projects, through the lens of the UN Sustainable Development Goals.

Thesis: An approach for quantifying the social impact of carbon-credit projects and a Gen-AI tool for UN SDG claims.

Supervisors: Prof. Farookh Hussain · Dr. Morteza Saberi.

01 — The gap

Two projects, identical carbon, very different lives.

A tonne of CO₂ avoided is a tonne, wherever it happens. But one project might bring a village clean cookstoves, jobs and schooling, while another barely touches the people around it — and today's carbon price cannot tell them apart. Capital follows the cheapest tonne, not the most good done. This is the blind spot the research sets out to close.

Conventional view

Carbon-only

  • One metric — tonnes of CO₂e avoided.
  • Blind to employment, health, education, equity.
  • Rewards cheap tonnes over real development.
  • Community benefit is an unverified claim.

This research

Carbon + social impact

  • A measured social-impact score beside the carbon.
  • Anchored in the 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals.
  • Explainable — every component is visible.
  • SDG claims verified by a Gen-AI auditor.

02 — The framework

Four components of social impact

The thesis defines a social-impact score as the interaction of four measurable components — not a single number, but a structure that captures how a project actually reaches a community.

Social Impact  =  Development Contribution × SDG Alignment × Community Impact × Multiplier Effect

DCR

Development Contribution

The project's emission-reduction contribution — its environmental weight, the baseline of what it delivers.

SDGA

SDG Alignment

How many of the 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals the project genuinely advances — its breadth of benefit.

CI

Community Impact

The human dividend — employment created and households reached — the depth of benefit on the ground.

ME

Multiplier Effect

The cascading reach of a project that advances several goals at once, rather than a single isolated outcome.

How the model and the formula work together

Machine learning estimates the inputs. The formula produces the output.

The two never compete on the same number — which is what keeps the method honest. Learning handles the parts that are hard to observe; the transparent formula does the principled aggregation.

Gen-AI auditor → SDGA

reads the project description and detects which SDGs it genuinely supports.

Random Forest → CI

predicts the employment a project will create (CV R² 0.97) — its community impact.

Emissions → DCR, breadth → ME

the development contribution and multiplier follow directly from the project.

Formula → Social Impact

combines the learned components into one transparent, auditable score.

03 — Modelling & validation

What the statistics actually showed

Beyond defining the framework, the study built and validated statistical models to quantify and predict social impact across the 161 projects — linear regression, random forests and gradient boosting, tuned by grid search and tested with 10-fold cross-validation (MSE primary, MAE for the skewed target).

Correlation with social impact

Development Contribution is the strongest single predictor (r = 0.61), followed by Community Impact (0.48) and the Multiplier Effect (0.41) — the same three levers the live model exposes.

Model comparison — cross-validated MSE (lower is better)

Random Forest won (MSE 0.1587), capturing the non-linear interactions between components; the boosting methods lagged here, and regularised linear models were held back by multicollinearity.

A note on interpretation — magnitude, not need

Why a small project in an under-developed country can still score low

The score measures the realized magnitude of social benefit — employment created and the breadth of SDGs advanced — not a region's need. Scale is the dominant lever: a micro project delivers less total benefit wherever it sits. Holding scale constant, the host-country level barely changes the score (an under-developed setting actually edges slightly ahead). The low scores some under-developed projects receive trace back to the data itself — in this dataset those projects are almost all micro or small scale, while the large projects cluster in developing economies. The model reflects that composition; it does not penalise a country for being poor. The honest limitation: this framework can under-value high-need, small-scale projects — a known equity blind spot, and a candidate for a need-adjusted variant in future work.
